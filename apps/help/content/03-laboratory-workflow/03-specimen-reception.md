## Why reception is a control, not a formality

Most laboratory errors are pre-analytical, and reception is the last point at
which one can be caught before it becomes a result. Once an unsuitable specimen
is analysed, the wrong answer looks exactly like a right one.

## Receiving a specimen

Sidebar → **Receiving**.

1. Select the specimen and its order.
2. Assess the **condition**: Acceptable, Marginal or Rejected.
3. Record temperature and volume if your procedures require it.
4. Set **status**: Accepted, Rejected, or Recollection required.
5. **Record receipt.**

Accepting moves the order to *Received*. Rejecting moves it to *Rejected* and
**requires a reason** — without one the requester cannot know what to do
differently.

## What to check

| Check | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **Identity on the tube matches the request** | An unlabelled or mismatched tube is unusable. Never relabel one. |
| **Correct tube type** | The additive changes the result. EDTA in a potassium sample gives a wildly wrong answer. |
| **Adequate volume** | Under-filled citrate tubes change the anticoagulant ratio and invalidate coagulation results. |
| **Haemolysis** | Releases intracellular contents. |
| **Clots in an anticoagulated tube** | Invalidates cell counts. |
| **Time since collection** | Some analytes change in the tube. |
| **Transport temperature** | Some require cold, others must not be chilled. |

## Haemolysis, specifically

Haemolysis is the commonest reason to reject, and it matters because red cells
contain very different concentrations from plasma:

| Analyte | Effect | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Potassium | Large false increase | Intracellular K⁺ is ~25× plasma |
| LDH | Large false increase | Abundant intracellularly |
| AST | False increase | Abundant intracellularly |
| Bilirubin | Interference | Optical, from haemoglobin colour |

A haemolysed potassium can read 6.5 mmol/L when the patient's is 4.0. That is
the difference between a routine result and an emergency call about a
life-threatening value that is not happening.

## Rejecting is the safer act

Rejecting a specimen feels unhelpful — someone must be bled again. Reporting a
result from an unsuitable specimen is worse: it is acted on, and nobody knows
it was wrong.

The rule: **if you would not trust the result yourself, do not release it into
the record.**

### Rejection criteria

Your laboratory's accepted reasons are configured at **Settings → Rejection
criteria**, grouped as Quantity, Quality, Labelling or Transport. Using the
configured list keeps rejections consistent and makes the pattern analysable —
a spike in "clotted" from one ward is a training issue you can act on.

## Marginal specimens

Sometimes a specimen is imperfect but the only one obtainable — a difficult
paediatric draw, a patient about to be discharged. Mark it **Marginal**, record
what is wrong, and add a comment to the report so the clinician can weigh it.

Never silently accept a marginal specimen. The clinician cannot allow for a
limitation nobody told them about.

## Telling the requester

Rejection is only useful if someone hears about it. Contact the requester
promptly: what was wrong, and what to send instead.
