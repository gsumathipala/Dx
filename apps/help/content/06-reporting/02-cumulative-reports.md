## What it is

**Reports → Cumulative report** builds a grid for one patient: tests down the
side, episodes across the top, values in the cells.

It is the right format whenever the question is *"what is happening to this
patient?"* rather than *"what is this result?"*.

## Why a series is more informative

A single result is judged against a population interval that, by construction,
excludes 5% of healthy people. A series is judged against the patient
themselves, and within-person variation is usually far smaller than
between-person variation.

A creatinine of 105 µmol/L sits inside a typical interval. If this patient has
run at 70 for two years, it represents a 50% rise, and it matters.

## Reading it

**Direction and rate.** Slow deterioration over months and an abrupt change in
two days suggest different processes.

**Group related analytes.** Urea and creatinine rising together suggests renal
function; creatinine rising alone with normal urea is more likely muscle
turnover or interference.

**Look for step changes.** An abrupt shift with no clinical explanation may be
pre-analytical — a different collection site, an infusion arm — or a method
change.

**Beware method changes.** A discontinuity when the laboratory changed analyser
or reagent lot is a property of the method, not the patient. This is why method
validation and change control records matter: they let you explain a step in a
patient's series years later.

## Printing

The cumulative report prints cleanly for a clinic letter or ward round. Use the
**Print** button.
