## What a nonconformance is

Anything that did not conform to a requirement: a failed QC run, an
unacceptable proficiency result, a mislabelled specimen, a missed turnaround, a
result reported wrongly, a complaint, a safety incident, a privacy breach.

> **ISO 15189:2022 §8.7** requires nonconformities to be identified, their
> causes evaluated, action taken to prevent recurrence, and the effectiveness of
> that action reviewed.

## Correction is not corrective action

This distinction is the whole point, and it is where most CAPA systems fail.

**Correction** fixes the immediate problem. The QC failed, so you recalibrated
and repeated it. The instrument now works.

**Corrective action** stops it happening again. *Why* did the calibration
drift? Because the reagent was stored above its temperature range. Because the
fridge alarm had been silenced. Because nobody was responsible for checking it.

A CAPA system that only records corrections produces a growing list of the same
incident, each individually closed.

## Raising one

**Nonconformances → New**. QC failures and unacceptable proficiency results open
one automatically.

| Field | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| Title, category, severity | Classification and triage |
| **Description** | What happened, factually |
| **Immediate action** | Containment — what you did at once |
| **Root cause** | Why it happened |
| **Corrective action** | What stops it recurring |
| **Preventive action** | What stops related problems |
| **Effectiveness check** | How you confirmed the fix worked |
| Patient impact | Whether results were affected |

## Finding a root cause

The commonest technique is **five whys** — keep asking why until you reach
something you can change:

> The result was wrong.
> *Why?* The analyser was out of calibration.
> *Why?* The calibration had expired.
> *Why?* Nobody noticed the due date.
> *Why?* Calibration due dates are not checked routinely.
> *Why?* There is no scheduled check and no owner.

The first answer is the instrument. The fifth is a missing process — and only
the fifth is fixable in a way that prevents recurrence.

**Stop at process, not at people.** "Operator error" is almost never a root
cause. The useful question is why the process allowed the error: was training
adequate, was the procedure clear, was the workload reasonable, was there a
check that would have caught it?

## Closing one

Dx will not let a nonconformance be closed until root cause, corrective action
and effectiveness check are all recorded. Closing without them would leave a
record that says something was fixed without saying what or how.

The **effectiveness check** is the part most often skipped: revisiting later to
confirm the problem stopped. A fix nobody verified is a hypothesis.

## Patient impact

If patient results were affected, say so and record how many. That triggers the
real work: identifying which results, deciding whether they changed care, and
issuing [amended reports](/help/reporting/amended-reports/) where they did.

A nonconformance that affected patients and says otherwise is the most serious
kind of incomplete record.
