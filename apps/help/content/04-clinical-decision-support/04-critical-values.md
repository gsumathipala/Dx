## What a critical value is

A result so far outside the expected range that it represents an **immediate
threat to life** unless something is done. Not merely abnormal — abnormal
enough that delay causes harm.

The concept dates to Lundberg in 1972, who coined "panic value" for results
demanding the clinician be told *now*, by voice, not by a report that might be
read tomorrow.

## Typical critical limits

Every laboratory sets its own, agreed with its clinicians. Representative
adult values:

| Analyte | Critical low | Critical high | Why it kills |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Potassium | < 2.5 mmol/L | > 6.5 mmol/L | Cardiac arrhythmia, arrest |
| Sodium | < 120 mmol/L | > 160 mmol/L | Cerebral oedema; seizures |
| Glucose | < 2.5 mmol/L | > 25 mmol/L | Coma; ketoacidosis |
| Calcium | < 1.6 mmol/L | > 3.5 mmol/L | Tetany; arrhythmia |
| Haemoglobin | < 70 g/L | > 200 g/L | Tissue hypoxia; hyperviscosity |
| Platelets | < 20 ×10⁹/L | > 1000 ×10⁹/L | Spontaneous haemorrhage; thrombosis |

Paediatric and neonatal limits differ substantially — Dx uses a demographic
critical limit where one is configured for the patient's age.

## The obligation

A critical value creates a duty to **communicate it to someone who can act**,
promptly, and to **record that you did**.

> **CAP GEN.41320** requires the laboratory to document critical value
> notification, including read-back of the result by the receiver.

## Why read-back

The receiver repeats the result back to you, and you confirm it is correct.

This exists because verbal communication fails in predictable ways.
"Potassium six point five" over a noisy ward telephone can be heard as "five
point six" — a normal value. The listener then does nothing, and the record
shows the laboratory called.

Read-back closes that gap: if they repeat it wrongly, you hear it and correct
it. The practice comes from aviation and nuclear operations for the same
reason.

## Tutorial: documenting a notification

### Step 1 — Find it

Critical values appear on your dashboard and under **Critical Values**, with
how long each has been waiting. Overdue ones are red.

### Step 2 — Telephone someone who can act

A named clinician responsible for the patient. Not a ward clerk, not a
voicemail. If you cannot reach the requesting clinician, escalate — the
consultant on call, the ward sister, the duty medical officer.

### Step 3 — State it clearly

Patient name and identifier, the test, the value with units, and that it is a
critical result.

### Step 4 — Ask them to read it back

Wait for it. Correct them if it is wrong.

### Step 5 — Record it

**Document** on the notification, and enter:

| Field | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Who you notified | A person's name, not "the ward" |
| Method | Telephone, in person, fax, secure message |
| **Read-back confirmed** | Required — you cannot close it otherwise |
| Notes | Anything relevant: repeat requested, sample queried |
| Escalated to | If you had to go beyond the requester |

Read-back confirmation is mandatory because without it there is no evidence the
result was received correctly.

## Escalation

If a notification is not documented within its window it is flagged overdue.

| Priority | Window |
| --- | --- |
| STAT | 30 minutes |
| Everything else | 60 minutes |

An overdue critical value is a serious operational failure, not a paperwork
lapse. The point is that someone somewhere has a dangerously abnormal result
and nobody has told anyone.

## Before you call

Take ten seconds to ask whether the result is real:

- **Is it consistent with the patient?** A potassium of 7.0 in a well
  outpatient is more likely haemolysis than hyperkalaemia.
- **Was the specimen suitable?** Haemolysis raises potassium dramatically.
- **Did QC pass?**
- **Does the rest of the panel fit?**

If you suspect a spurious result, say so when you call — and request a repeat.
Do not simply withhold it: "I thought it was wrong" is not a defence if it was
not.

A critical value you decide is spurious is still documented, with your reasoning.
