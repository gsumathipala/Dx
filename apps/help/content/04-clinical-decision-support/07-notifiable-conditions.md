## The obligation

Some diagnoses must be reported to a public health authority by law, within a
stated time, regardless of patient consent. The laboratory is frequently the
first to know, and in many jurisdictions is a designated notifier in its own
right.

The purpose is outbreak control. A single case of measles or cholera is an
early warning; recognising it days later is the difference between a contained
cluster and an epidemic.

## Typical conditions and windows

Specifics vary by jurisdiction — use your own list — but the shape is common:

| Urgency | Examples | Typical window |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Immediate** | Cholera, plague, viral haemorrhagic fever, diphtheria, anthrax | Hours; often by telephone |
| **24 hours** | Measles, meningococcal disease, typhoid, acute polio | 24 hours |
| **Routine** | Tuberculosis, hepatitis B and C, salmonellosis, campylobacter | 3–7 days |

Immediate-notification conditions usually require a telephone call as well as a
written notification. Do not rely on a system entry alone for those.

## How Dx tracks it

**Settings → Notifiable conditions** links each condition to the tests that
detect it, names the reporting body, and sets the statutory window.

When a linked test is resulted, a notification is raised automatically and
appears under **Epidemiology**. Each carries its deadline; overdue ones are
flagged.

> **All** matching conditions are raised, not just the first. One organism can
> satisfy several reporting obligations — a *Salmonella typhi* isolate may be
> notifiable both as typhoid and as an enteric pathogen.

## Working a notification

1. **Review** it — confirm the result supports the diagnosis.
2. **Report** to the named body by their required route.
3. **Record** it: mark submitted and enter the reference they give you.

The reference number matters. It is the evidence the notification was made, and
what you quote if the authority later queries the case.

## What is disclosed, and why consent is not needed

Statutory notification is a permitted disclosure without patient consent —
HIPAA §164.512(b) covers public health activities, and GDPR Article 9(2)(i)
covers public interest in public health.

It is still **recorded as a disclosure** in the accounting. Permitted is not the
same as unrecorded: a patient asking for an accounting of disclosures is
entitled to see it.

Disclose the minimum the authority requires. That is usually the identity, the
organism and the specimen date — not the whole record.

## If you are unsure

Report it. Over-notification is a minor administrative burden; under-notifying
a communicable disease can cost lives and is, in most jurisdictions, an
offence. Ask the public health body — they would far rather answer a question
than miss a case.
